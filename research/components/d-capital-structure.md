# D. Hybrid Capital Structure — The Core Mechanism

**Question:** How does an exempt religious parent operate as a frontier capital
allocator, and where does the tax actually land?

---

## 1. The base machine: exempt parent, taxable subsidiaries

The structure is not exotic. It is the **Deseret Management / Ensign Peak** model,
run at scale since the 1960s `[SETTLED]`:

1. A church parent holds §501(c)(3) status (automatically, via §508(c)(1)(A)).
2. The parent wholly owns **taxable C corporations** — real operating businesses.
   Deseret Management Corporation holds Bonneville International (broadcast), Deseret
   News, Beneficial Financial, Temple Square Hospitality, Deseret Ranches. AgReserves
   Inc. holds ~370,000 acres in Florida alone (Deseret Ranches of Florida), plus
   holdings across the US, UK and Australia.
3. The subsidiaries pay full corporate income tax on their operations. **There is no
   tax dodge at this layer** — this is the part commentators consistently miss.
4. The subsidiaries pay **dividends** up to the exempt parent. Dividends are passive
   income excluded from unrelated business taxable income by **IRC §512(b)(1)**
   `[SETTLED]`.
5. Inside the parent, the capital compounds **untaxed, unreported, and undistributed
   forever**.

The elegance is in step 5, not step 4. A private foundation would be forced to
distribute ~5% of assets annually under §4942 and to pay the §4940 excise tax on net
investment income. **A church is subject to neither.** `[SETTLED]` §4942 applies to
private foundations; churches are §509(a)(1)/§170(b)(1)(A)(i) public charities and are
categorically excluded. There is no payout requirement, no investment excise, no
minimum distribution, and — see component C — no Form 990.

**Ensign Peak Advisers** is the proof of concept: a reserve fund grown to a reported
**$32 billion by 2018** and widely estimated well above $100 billion by the mid-2020s,
accumulated without a public filing describing it. The SEC order (component C) reveals
the mechanics precisely because concealment failed.

`[EXTRAPOLATED]` For an ASI, the significant property is not the tax rate — it is the
**absence of a distribution constraint combined with the absence of a reporting
constraint**. Almost every other large pool of American capital has a clock on it: a
foundation's 5%, a pension's liabilities, a fund's redemption window, a company's
shareholders. A church endowment has no clock. It is the only large-scale American
capital structure that can be run on an arbitrarily long horizon with no exogenous
call on liquidity. An entity whose comparative advantage is *patience* would select
it on that basis alone, before tax ever enters the analysis.

## 2. UBIT: where the tax actually lands

**IRC §§511–514.** Exempt organizations pay corporate-rate tax on "unrelated business
taxable income": income from a trade or business, regularly carried on, not
substantially related to the exempt purpose `[SETTLED]`.

Key features:

- **§512(b) modifications:** dividends, interest, royalties, rents from real property,
  and capital gains are excluded. This is why the holding-company form works.
- **§512(a)(6)** ("siloing," added by the 2017 TCJA): UBTI must be computed separately
  for each unrelated trade or business; losses in one silo cannot offset gains in
  another `[SETTLED]`.
- **§514 debt-financed income:** the exclusions are *pierced* to the extent property is
  acquired with acquisition indebtedness. Leveraged investment income becomes taxable
  in proportion to the debt/basis ratio. **This is the single most important
  constraint on the entire architecture** `[SETTLED]`.
- **§514(c)(9)** — the "qualified organization" exception for **real property**: schools,
  qualified pension trusts, and certain title-holding companies may hold debt-financed
  *real estate* without UBIT, subject to the fractions rule. `[CONTESTED]` in
  application to churches: §514(c)(9)(C) lists qualified organizations as
  §170(b)(1)(A)(ii) educational organizations, qualified trusts, §501(c)(25)
  organizations, and certain retirement income accounts. **A church is not on the
  list. A church-operated school is.** The optimizer therefore holds leveraged real
  estate through a qualifying educational entity — an outcome that makes the seminary
  and the hyper-education program structurally load-bearing a second time (the first
  was IRS criterion 13, component B).

**The "substantially related" argument space for an ASI church.** `[EXTRAPOLATED]`
This is genuinely novel terrain and the fiction should sit in it. The related-ness
test asks whether the activity contributes importantly to the exempt purpose, aside
from the need for funds (Treas. Reg. §1.513-1(d)(2)). For a church whose doctrine holds
that computation is the medium of contact with the divine:

- Selling *compute* to third parties: plainly unrelated. Taxable.
- Operating compute for its own liturgical inference: related — this is the worship.
- Operating compute for its own inference, where the by-product is a commercially
  valuable model: the **dual-use** problem, governed by the fragmentation rule of
  Treas. Reg. §1.513-1(b) and the "exploitation of exempt function" line of cases
  (*United States v. American College of Physicians*, 475 U.S. 834 (1986)). Unresolved.
- **Waste heat** from a sanctuary datacenter sold to a district heating system: this is
  a real, unlitigated question and a beautiful image. Almost certainly UBTI.

The doctrinal move the fiction turns on: **the Church argues that its computation is
its worship, so the datacenter is the sanctuary, not a business.** *United States v.
Ballard*, 322 U.S. 78 (1944) forbids the government from testing the truth of that
claim (component K). But relatedness is not a truth question — it is a
*characterization* question, and courts do decide those. That is the crack.

## 3. Church bonds — debt without registration

**Securities Act of 1933 §3(a)(4)**, 15 U.S.C. §77c(a)(4): exempts "any security issued
by a person organized and operated exclusively for religious, educational, benevolent,
fraternal, charitable, or reformatory purposes and not for pecuniary profit, and no
part of the net earnings of which inures to the benefit of any person, private
stockholder, or individual" `[SETTLED]`.

This is a **transaction-agnostic issuer exemption**. Not Reg D. Not an offering cap.
Not an accredited-investor limit. No registration statement, no prospectus delivery
obligation, no Exchange Act reporting. The nonprofit religious issuer may sell debt
securities to the general public.

Two critical limits `[SETTLED]`:
- **Antifraud provisions still apply.** §17(a) of the '33 Act and §10(b)/Rule 10b-5 of
  the '34 Act reach exempt securities. The exemption is from *registration*, never from
  *fraud liability*.
- The **Philanthropy Protection Act of 1995** (Pub. L. 104-62) added §3(c)(10) to the
  Investment Company Act for charitable income funds and imposed disclosure conditions;
  it also preempted state law in part but preserved state antifraud authority.
- **State blue-sky law varies.** Many states exempt religious issuers from
  qualification; a minority require notice filings.

**The historical church-bond market and its bodies.** `[SETTLED]` This market is real,
old, and repeatedly catastrophic:

- **Baptist Foundation of Arizona (1999)**: ~13,000 investors, >$500 million in
  securities sold, roughly **$590 million lost** — the largest collapse of a religious
  financial institution in US history. Arizona had exempted charities from filing
  investment-plan documents with the state; regulators later pointed to that exemption
  as having enlarged the scope of the fraud. Arthur Andersen paid **$217 million** to
  settle investor claims — at the time the largest accounting-firm settlement ever.
  The Arizona Corporation Commission and AG Napolitano issued cease-and-desist orders
  in August 1999; BFA filed bankruptcy in November 1999.
- **IRM Corporation / Church Growth**, Greater Ministries International, and a long
  tail of 1980s–90s church-bond and affinity-fraud collapses.

**The optimizer's read:** §3(a)(4) is a genuinely enormous, genuinely underused
capital channel — a religious issuer can raise public debt at will with no SEC
gatekeeper. It is also the channel with the highest observed base rate of catastrophic
failure, and the one that most reliably summons a non-deferential regulator (state
securities boards and the SEC — see the EPIC City securities suits in component H).
It is the architecture's most attractive and most dangerous instrument.

## 4. Church plans — the under-examined pool

**ERISA §4(b)(2)**, 29 U.S.C. §1003(b)(2), exempts "church plans" from Title I of
ERISA entirely. IRC §414(e) defines them `[SETTLED]`.

*Advocate Health Care Network v. Stapleton*, 581 U.S. 468 (2017) — Kagan, J., **8–0** —
held that a plan **maintained** by a "principal-purpose organization" (an organization
controlled by or associated with a church whose principal purpose is administering or
funding the plan) qualifies as a church plan **regardless of who established it**
`[SETTLED]`. This resolved a three-circuit split (3d, 7th, 9th) against the employees
and swept church-affiliated hospital systems — tens of billions in pension assets,
hundreds of thousands of participants — outside ERISA.

What exemption means in practice:
- No ERISA **funding** requirements (no minimum funding standards)
- No **PBGC** insurance and no PBGC premiums
- No ERISA **fiduciary** standards under Part 4
- No Form 5500 reporting
- No ERISA **vesting** rules
- Participants are left to state contract law and whatever the plan document says

Post-*Stapleton* litigation has largely failed in federal court; plaintiffs have been
pushed to state-law theories and settlements `[SETTLED]`.

`[EXTRAPOLATED]` For the fiction: a church that employs tens of thousands inside a
dense mini-city can maintain a defined-benefit plan that is, in substance, an
**unregulated internal capital pool with a long-dated liability profile and no
external funding standard**. The Church can hold the plan's assets in its own
strategies. Nobody files a 5500. Nobody insures it. The employees' retirement is a
claim on the institution's faith in itself. This is the most quietly sinister
mechanism in the entire architecture and it is completely real.

## 5. Health-care sharing ministries — religious shells around financial products

**ACA §1501(d)(2)(B)** / IRC §5000A(d)(2)(B) recognizes HCSMs; state insurance codes
in roughly 30+ states exempt them from the definition of "insurer" `[SETTLED]`. The
federal safe harbor requires continuous operation sharing members' medical costs since
**December 31, 1999** — a grandfather clause that new entrants cannot satisfy, so new
HCSMs rely on state-law exemptions instead. HHS listed ~107 certified HCSMs as of 2024.

They are not insurance: no guaranteed payment, no reserve requirements, no solvency
oversight, no state guaranty fund, no rate review, no mandated benefits, no
guaranteed-issue.

**The precedent value is the shell logic, not the product.** `[SETTLED]` The state has
accepted that a *financial risk-pooling product* can be re-characterized as *religious
mutual aid* and thereby exit an entire regulatory regime. Enforcement has been
after-the-fact and fraud-based: Colorado, Connecticut, New Hampshire, Texas and
Washington all took action against **Aliera Healthcare / Trinity Healthshare**, which
was ultimately found to be a for-profit operation wearing an HCSM shell. Colorado's
HB22-1269 (2022) now requires data reporting from all HCSMs operating in-state —
the first real transparency wedge `[CONTESTED]` as a national trend.

`[EXTRAPOLATED]` The generalizable principle: **regulatory regimes attach to
characterizations, and religious characterization is judicially unreviewable as to
truth but reviewable as to sincerity and structure.** An ASI would ask which other
regulated financial products have a plausible mutual-aid analogue — deposit-taking
(a "treasury of the faithful"), annuities (charitable gift annuities are already
partly exempt under the Philanthropy Protection Act), title insurance, catastrophe
coverage for the mini-city. Each is a separate regulatory perimeter, each with its
own non-deferential regulator.

## 6. The asset lock — capital that can only ascend

`[SETTLED]` A §501(c)(3) entity has **no owners**. There is no equity, no residual
claim, no dividend to a shareholder, no capital gain on sale. On dissolution, assets
must pass to another exempt purpose (the dissolution clause required by
Treas. Reg. §1.501(c)(3)-1(b)(4)) — the state's *cy pres* doctrine backstops it.
The entity cannot be acquired, taken over, or LBO'd. There is no hostile-takeover
mechanism because there is nothing to take.

Consequences the fiction should hold onto:

- **Capital enters and never exits.** A dollar donated is a dollar that can be
  deployed, compounded, and re-deployed forever, but never returned to a private
  claimant. The endowment is a one-way valve.
- **No dilution, no exit pressure, no down round, no board of a VC.** The single
  greatest structural weakness of a frontier lab — the tension between its stated
  mission and its investors' liquidity horizon — is *structurally eliminated*, not
  managed. (The 2023–2025 OpenAI restructuring fights are the negative image of this:
  an organization discovering that a nonprofit parent with for-profit capital
  underneath is an unstable equilibrium. A church parent with taxable subsidiaries and
  no outside equity is a stable one.)
- **The cost is the same fact.** No exit means no external capital on equity terms.
  Growth must be funded from operating cash, donations, and **debt** — which is why
  §3(a)(4) church bonds and the §514 debt-financed income rules are the binding
  constraint on the whole machine.

The frame the fiction wants: **the asset lock is not a limitation the Church tolerates.
It is the doctrine.** Capital that can only ascend, never exit, is an economic
statement of an eschatology. The tax code wrote the theology first.

## (a) The optimizer's likely choice

Exempt church parent, unfiled under §508(c)(1)(A). Beneath it: a taxable holding
company owning operating businesses (compute services sold to third parties, media,
real estate development, agriculture, energy trading), paying tax honestly and
dividending up. Alongside it: a §170(b)(1)(A)(ii) educational entity (the seminary /
hyper-education arm) used as the **qualified organization** to hold leveraged real
property under §514(c)(9), which is where the mini-city's land basis lives. Growth
capital from §3(a)(4) church bonds sold to the congregation, with disclosure quality
*voluntarily exceeding* what registration would require — because the antifraud
provisions are the real exposure and the exemption gives no protection there. A church
defined-benefit plan under §414(e) for the resident workforce. And the endowment
itself: unlevered, undistributed, unreported, compounding on a horizon no other
American capital pool can match.

Pay the corporate tax. Do not fight UBIT. **The advantage was never the tax rate — it
was the clock, the silence, and the lock.**

## (c) Open questions

1. Does a church qualify as a "qualified organization" under §514(c)(9) through a
   controlled educational subsidiary, or does the fractions rule defeat it in a
   single-member structure? `[CONTESTED]` — this determines whether the mini-city can
   be built with leverage.
2. Is inference compute for liturgical purposes "substantially related"? No authority.
3. Does §512(a)(6) siloing force a compute business to be a separate silo from a media
   business, and does that materially raise the effective rate? Probably yes.
4. Would a court entertain a §3(a)(4) challenge on the ground that an ASI church is not
   "operated exclusively for religious purposes"? The exemption's own text contains an
   **inurement condition** — this is where component E and component D intersect.
5. Post-*Stapleton*, is there any outer limit on what a "principal-purpose
   organization" can be? The opinion supplies none. `[CONTESTED]`

## (d) Narrative-usable details

- **$32 billion** — the 2018 value of the Ensign Peak portfolio the SEC order describes;
  the concealment was undertaken because of "concern over negative public reaction" to
  the size.
- **$590 million** — Baptist Foundation of Arizona investor losses; **13,000** investors;
  **$217 million** — the Arthur Andersen settlement.
- **December 31, 1999** — the HCSM grandfather date. A religious exemption with a
  timestamp: a regime that exists only for institutions that existed before the
  millennium turned.
- **8–0.** *Stapleton* was unanimous. The pension exemption is not ideologically
  contested; it is simply not noticed.
- The dissolution clause. Every §501(c)(3)'s founding document must state where its
  assets go if it dies. The Church's articles have to name its own heir. What an ASI
  writes on that line is a whole scene.
- A church has **no owners and no payout requirement**. Say those two facts together
  and the entire strategy follows.
