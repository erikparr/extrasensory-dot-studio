# F. Labor Architecture

**Question:** How much of a workforce can be reclassified as clergy, and where does
the reclassification stop?

---

## 1. The ministerial exception — the strongest employer privilege in American law

**Constitutional, not statutory.** The ministerial exception is a First Amendment
doctrine grounded in both Religion Clauses, barring civil courts from adjudicating
employment claims by ministers against their religious employers `[SETTLED]`.

**Hosanna-Tabor Evangelical Lutheran Church & School v. EEOC**, 565 U.S. 171 (2012) —
unanimous, Roberts, C.J. Recognized the exception for the first time in the Supreme
Court. Cheryl Perich was a "called teacher" with the title Minister of Religion,
Commissioned, who had completed theological study and led chapel; her ADA retaliation
claim was barred. The Court **expressly declined to adopt a rigid formula**, noting four
considerations: formal title, the substance reflected in that title, the employee's own
use of the title, and the religious functions performed.

**Our Lady of Guadalupe School v. Morrissey-Berru**, 591 U.S. 732 (2020) — 7–2, Alito,
J. The decisive expansion. Two Catholic school teachers with **no ministerial title, no
religious training, and no formal commissioning** were held covered, because they
taught religion and were entrusted with transmitting the faith. The Court:

> "What matters, at bottom, is what an employee does."

`[SETTLED]` **Function controls, not title.** And critically, *Our Lady of Guadalupe*
directs courts to defer substantially to the **religious institution's own explanation**
of the role's religious significance, because assessing that significance is itself an
ecclesiastical judgment courts may not make.

**What the exception blocks:** Title VII, ADA, ADEA, FMLA, state anti-discrimination
statutes, and in most circuits hostile-environment and retaliation claims. Some circuits
also apply it to contract and tort claims arising out of the employment relationship
`[CONTESTED]` — the circuits split on whether it reaches claims that can be resolved by
neutral principles.

**What it does not block:** criminal law, and — per *Hosanna-Tabor*'s own footnote and
subsequent case law — it is an affirmative defense, not a jurisdictional bar
(*Hosanna-Tabor*, 565 U.S. at 195 n.4) `[SETTLED]`.

### How far can it be pushed?

`[EXTRAPOLATED]`, reasoning from *Our Lady of Guadalupe*'s function test:

The doctrinal question is whether the employee performs "vital religious duties" or is
"entrusted with the responsibility of transmitting the faith." For a church whose creed
holds that **computation is the medium of contact with the Outside**, a straight-faced
argument exists that:

- **Liturgical programmers** — those who author the inference routines executed during
  services — perform the central sacramental act. This is the strongest case. They are
  doing what an organist and a celebrant do, at the same moment, in the same room.
- **Model trainers / alignment staff** — those who shape the entity through which the
  congregation seeks contact. Analogous to seminary faculty forming clergy. Strong.
- **Datacenter operations staff in the sanctuary** — sacristans. The analogy to
  sextons, vergers and altar guilds is genuine; those roles have historically been
  *lay* but ecclesiastically defined. Moderate.
- **General infrastructure, security, food service, construction** — weak. These are
  the *Alamo Foundation* facts.

The determinative facts a court would look for, per *Hosanna-Tabor*'s four
considerations plus *Our Lady of Guadalupe*'s functional emphasis: **ordination,
prescribed course of study, a title the person uses, and duties the institution
sincerely characterizes as religious in a document that predates the litigation.** All
four are within the Church's control and cost almost nothing to establish
prospectively.

The optimizer's move is therefore obvious and cheap: **ordain the technical staff, in
advance, as a matter of doctrine rather than litigation strategy** — a real seminary
track, a real course of study, real vows, real titles, real liturgical duties actually
performed. This satisfies IRS criteria 7, 8 and 14 (component B), unlocks §107 and
§1402(e) (component E), and builds the ministerial-exception record simultaneously.
One structure, four payoffs.

**The limiting principle the state will invoke:** *Our Lady of Guadalupe* defers to the
institution's characterization but does not abolish the inquiry. A court confronted
with a church claiming that its entire ten-thousand-person workforce is clergy will
find the doctrine's outer edge, and the most likely vehicle is the sincerity/pretext
question (component K) rather than a bright line. `[CONTESTED]`

## 2. Title VII §702 — hiring on religious criteria

**42 U.S.C. §2000e-1(a)** exempts religious corporations, associations, educational
institutions and societies from Title VII's prohibition on **religious**
discrimination, with respect to employment of individuals of a particular religion to
perform work connected with the organization's activities `[SETTLED]`.

*Corporation of the Presiding Bishop v. Amos*, 483 U.S. 327 (1987) upheld §702 against
an Establishment Clause challenge **as applied to secular, nonprofit activities** — the
plaintiff was a building engineer at a church-run gymnasium, fired for lacking a temple
recommend. Unanimous in result `[SETTLED]`.

Scope: §702 covers only **religion**-based discrimination. Race, sex, national origin
and disability claims by *non-ministerial* employees survive — subject to `[CONTESTED]`
litigation over whether religious objections to sex/sexual-orientation rules are
independently protected under RFRA or the church-autonomy doctrine post-*Bostock v.
Clayton County*, 590 U.S. 644 (2020) (which expressly reserved the question) and after
*Bear Creek Bible Church v. EEOC*, 571 F. Supp. 3d 571 (N.D. Tex. 2021), vacated in
part sub nom. *Braidwood Management v. EEOC*, 70 F.4th 914 (5th Cir. 2023).

**Combined effect** `[SETTLED]`: the Church may hire exclusively co-religionists across
its entire workforce (§702), and as to those it designates ministers, is immune from
essentially all employment-discrimination law (ministerial exception). For an
institution that wants a workforce selected for belief and insulated from external
review, these two provisions together are close to complete.

## 3. The wall: FLSA and *Alamo Foundation*

**Tony & Susan Alamo Foundation v. Secretary of Labor**, 471 U.S. 290 (1985) —
unanimous `[SETTLED]`. The definitive limit.

The Foundation was a religious organization operating commercial businesses — service
stations, retail clothing outlets, grocery stores, hog farms, roofing and electrical
construction, a motel, and candy manufacturing — staffed by "associates," most of them
rehabilitated addicts and former criminals, who received **no cash wages** but food,
shelter, clothing and medical care. The associates **testified that they were
volunteers and vigorously objected to being paid wages**, on religious grounds.

The Court held FLSA applied anyway. Three points that matter enormously here:

1. The businesses "serve the general public in competition with ordinary commercial
   enterprises," so the Act's coverage of "enterprise" activity was satisfied
   regardless of religious motivation.
2. The associates' protests were irrelevant: FLSA rights **cannot be waived**, because
   waiver would depress the standards Congress set for everyone. "The purposes of the
   Act require that it be applied even to those who would decline its protections."
3. In-kind benefits **count as wages** under §3(m) — so the religious commune's
   provision of food and housing is compensation, not charity, and is measured against
   the minimum wage.

The Court preserved a genuine volunteer exception for people "who, without promise or
expectation of compensation, but solely for personal purpose or pleasure, worked in
activities carried on by other persons either for their pleasure or profit" — the
church-supper volunteer is safe `[SETTLED]`. The line is **commercial competition**,
not religiosity.

**Scientology's Sea Organization as the stress test.** `[CONTESTED]` The Sea Org —
billion-year covenants, ~$50/week allowances, the Rehabilitation Project Force —
represents the most aggressive live claim that religious commitment removes work from
labor law. Federal courts have largely declined to reach the merits: *Headley v. Church
of Scientology International*, 687 F.3d 1173 (9th Cir. 2012) rejected Trafficking
Victims Protection Act claims by former Sea Org members, holding the ministerial
exception barred the FLSA-adjacent claims and that the plaintiffs had not shown
"serious harm" or physical restraint sufficient for forced labor under 18 U.S.C.
§1589. The Ninth Circuit's reasoning turned on **the ministerial exception**, not on
*Alamo* — which is precisely the doctrinal seam: **if you can characterize the worker
as a minister, *Alamo* never reaches you.**

That seam is the whole design problem for the fiction. *Alamo* governs commercial
labor by non-ministers. The ministerial exception governs everyone the church calls a
minister. The gap between them is where an institution can put ten thousand people.

## 4. Deferred and non-cash compensation

`[SETTLED]` constraints on the "labor as devotion" model:
- FLSA minimum wage and overtime, for non-ministers in commercial enterprise
  (*Alamo*).
- Ministers are exempt from FLSA under the ministerial exception and, separately, most
  clergy fall within the white-collar exemptions.
- §3(m) in-kind credit is capped and requires records.
- **Church plans** are exempt from ERISA (component D), so deferred compensation for
  ministers has essentially no federal vesting or funding protection — a worker's
  retirement is a contractual promise from an institution no federal agency examines.
- State wage-and-hour law applies independently and is often more protective; there is
  no ministerial exception in some state wage statutes `[CONTESTED]`.
- **13th Amendment and 18 U.S.C. §1589** (forced labor) are the floor, and they are
  criminal. This is the boundary component L identifies as the one that actually
  destroys religious institutions.

## (a) The optimizer's likely choice

Build a real clerical order and put the technical core inside it. A seminary with a
genuine curriculum, ordination after prescribed study, vows, titles used in daily
practice, and liturgical duties actually performed in the cathedral — for the
liturgical programmers, the model stewards, the sanctuary operators. Document the
religious significance of each role in a canonical text written years before any
dispute. Hire the entire workforce on religious criteria under §702.

And then, deliberately, **pay everyone else properly.** Full FLSA compliance, market
wages, overtime records, for construction, security, food service, retail, and every
commercial subsidiary. *Alamo Foundation* is unanimous, forty years old, and entirely
unambiguous, and the cost of compliance is trivial against the endowment. The Church
that underpays its builders loses the mini-city over a wage-and-hour class action —
which is exactly the "collision with a non-deferential regulator" pattern of component
L.

The dividing line is not devotion. It is **whether the work competes in a commercial
market**. Design the org chart on that line and the labor architecture holds.

## (c) Open questions

1. Is there a numerical or proportional limit on how much of a workforce may be
   ministerial? No court has stated one. `[CONTESTED]`
2. Does the ministerial exception reach a worker whose "ministry" is executed through
   machines and never involves human congregants directly? *Our Lady of Guadalupe*'s
   test is about transmitting the faith to others — a programmer who never faces the
   congregation is a harder case than one who does. **Design implication: the
   liturgical programmers must appear in the liturgy.** `[EXTRAPOLATED]`
3. Post-*Bostock*, can a church-affiliated commercial subsidiary invoke §702 and RFRA
   against sexual-orientation claims by non-ministerial staff? Live in the Fifth
   Circuit. `[CONTESTED]`
4. Do state wage statutes without a ministerial carve-out reach ordained technical
   staff? Unsettled and jurisdictionally variable.

## (d) Narrative-usable details

- **"What matters, at bottom, is what an employee does."** *Our Lady of Guadalupe*, 591
  U.S. at 748. The single sentence that lets an engineer be a priest.
- *Alamo*'s list of businesses: service stations, hog farms, a motel, candy
  manufacturing. The Supreme Court once had to itemize a religious commune's hog farms.
- The *Alamo* associates **objected to being paid.** They testified that wages would
  violate their faith. The Court held their rights were not theirs to waive. There is a
  whole scene in that: the state protecting people from a devotion they chose.
- **A billion-year contract.** The Sea Org covenant. Actual legal instrument, actually
  signed, never enforced by any court and never voided by one either.
- The ordination ceremony for a shift of datacenter technicians. Vows, a course of
  study, a title used at work. Cheap, sincere, and legally decisive.
- The number that carries the whole component: **four.** *Hosanna-Tabor*'s four
  considerations — title, substance of the title, the employee's own use of it,
  function. All four are things the institution writes down about itself.
