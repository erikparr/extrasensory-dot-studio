## 1. The prohibition

**IRC §501(c)(3)**: an exempt organization must not "participate in, or intervene in
(including the publishing or distributing of statements), any political campaign on
behalf of (or in opposition to) any candidate for public office" `[SETTLED]`.

Added by floor amendment from Senator Lyndon B. Johnson in 1954 — hence "the Johnson
Amendment." It is an **absolute** prohibition (unlike the lobbying limit, which is a
"no substantial part" test under §501(c)(3) and the §501(h) expenditure election, which
churches are **statutorily barred from making** under §501(h)(5)) `[SETTLED]`.

Sanctions: revocation of exemption, and/or the §4955 excise tax on political
expenditures (10% on the organization, 2.5% on managers; 100% / 50% second-tier).

**Rev. Rul. 2007-41** is the operative framework `[SETTLED]`. It sets out 21 fact
patterns across six categories — voter education and registration, individual
activity by religious leaders, candidate appearances, issue advocacy, business
activity, and websites — and applies a facts-and-circumstances test. Its core
distinctions:

- A minister may endorse **in their individual capacity**, but not at official church
  functions, in official publications, or using church resources.
- Voter guides are permissible if genuinely neutral, covering a broad range of issues,
  and not comparing positions to the organization's own.
- Candidate appearances are permissible if all candidates are invited on equal terms,
  or if the candidate appears in a non-candidate capacity with no campaign references
  and no fundraising.
- Issue advocacy becomes intervention when it identifies a candidate, is timed to an
  election, targets voters in the relevant jurisdiction, or references voting records
  on the issue.

## 2. The one revocation

**Branch Ministries v. Rossotti**, 211 F.3d 137 (D.C. Cir. 2000) `[SETTLED]`.

Four days before the 1992 election, the Church at Pierce Creek (Binghamton, NY) placed
full-page advertisements in *USA Today* and the *Washington Times* headed **"Christian
Beware: Do not put the economy ahead of the Ten Commandments"**, stating that Bill
Clinton's positions violated Biblical precepts and asking "How then can we vote for Bill
Clinton?" The ads solicited "tax-deductible donations" to defray their cost. The IRS
revoked the church's exemption in 1995.

The D.C. Circuit affirmed, rejecting free-exercise, RFRA, selective-prosecution and
equal-protection challenges. Its most-quoted reasoning: the burden was minimal because
the church could restructure — establish a §501(c)(4) affiliate for its political
speech, or forgo the exemption. Because it was a church, it had **never filed for a
determination letter** (§508(c)(1)(A) — component B), so the practical effect of
revocation was limited: the court noted the church could continue operating and, absent
a determination letter, the revocation's tangible consequence was mainly the loss of
donor deductibility.

**This is, to date, the only revocation of a church's exemption for political campaign
intervention in the statute's seventy-two-year history** `[SETTLED]`. The enforcement
record is one case.

## 3. What happened in 2025–2026

**July 2025 — the joint filing.** In *National Religious Broadcasters v. Long*, No.
6:24-cv-00311 (E.D. Tex.), NRB and two Texas churches (Sand Springs Church and First
Baptist Church Waskom) challenged the Johnson Amendment. The IRS and plaintiffs filed a
**joint motion for entry of a consent judgment** in which the IRS agreed the Amendment
should not be construed to reach speech by a house of worship **to its own
congregation, in connection with religious services, through its customary channels of
communication, on matters of faith, concerning electoral politics viewed through the
lens of religious faith** `[SETTLED]` as to the filing's content. The IRS analogized
such speech to "a family discussion concerning candidates." This was widely — and
inaccurately — reported as the IRS "allowing churches to endorse candidates."

**March 31, 2026 — rejection.** Judge **J. Campbell Barker** (E.D. Tex., Tyler
Division) **declined to enter the consent judgment and dismissed for lack of
jurisdiction** `[SETTLED]`. The grounds were jurisdictional, not substantive:

- The **Tax Anti-Injunction Act**, 26 U.S.C. §7421(a), bars suits "for the purpose of
  restraining the assessment or collection of any tax."
- The **Declaratory Judgment Act**, 28 U.S.C. §2201(a), excepts controversies "with
  respect to Federal taxes" from declaratory relief.

The court therefore never reached the First Amendment question, and the proposed
carve-out never took effect. NRB (general counsel **Michael Farris**) announced it would
appeal to the Fifth Circuit, arguing that an Anti-Injunction Act exception applies
`[CONTESTED]`.

**April 3, 2026 — Treasury's response.** On Good Friday, Treasury announced it would
issue **formal guidance** to religious organizations, promising "clear, administrable
standards for houses of worship, including how the law applies to certain
communications made within the context of religious services" `[SETTLED]` as to the
announcement.

**As of August 2026:**
- The Johnson Amendment **remains in force** and applies to the November 3, 2026
  election `[SETTLED]`.
- The item remains on the **Treasury/IRS Priority Guidance Plan**; **no proposed
  regulation has been published and nothing has been finalized** `[SETTLED]` as of this
  research date.
- The *NRB v. Long* appeal is pending `[CONTESTED]`.

`[EXTRAPOLATED]` — the three plausible near-term states, for plotting purposes:
1. **Guidance issues as a revenue ruling or proposed regulation** codifying the
   congregational-speech carve-out. Most likely. Would be litigated immediately by
   FFRF, Campaign Legal Center, and state AGs on Establishment Clause and APA grounds.
2. **Fifth Circuit revives *NRB*** on an Anti-Injunction Act exception, and the merits
   are reached in 2027–28.
3. **Nothing happens** and the status quo — a prohibition with a one-case enforcement
   record — persists indefinitely, which is functionally what has existed since 2000.

**The optimizer's read on all three:** the *formal* rule matters far less than the
*enforcement rate*. One revocation in seventy-two years is the operative fact. But note
the asymmetry that makes this dangerous rather than free: revocation is not the only
sanction. **§4955 excise taxes**, state AG action, media exposure, and — most
importantly — the reputational conversion of the Church from "religious institution" to
"political actor" are all live. Component L's lesson applies: churches do not fall on
doctrine; they fall when they hand a non-deferential actor a reason to look.

## 4. The affiliate architecture

`[SETTLED]` The standard, entirely lawful structure for an exempt organization with
political capacity:

```
   §501(c)(3) CHURCH ──────────────── no campaign intervention, ever
        │                             lobbying: "no substantial part" test
        │                             §501(h) election unavailable (§501(h)(5))
        │  grants (restricted, at cost, arm's length,
        │  documented; never for campaign purposes)
        ▼
   §501(c)(4) SOCIAL WELFARE ORG ─── may lobby without limit
        │                             may engage in campaign activity so long as it is
        │                             not the organization's PRIMARY activity
        │                             contributions NOT deductible
        │                             §527(f) tax on political expenditures
        ▼
   §527 PAC / SUPER PAC ───────────── unlimited express advocacy
                                      full FEC disclosure
```

Requirements for the wall to hold: separate boards (or at least a majority of
non-overlapping directors), separate books, separate bank accounts, written
cost-sharing agreements at fair market value, no §501(c)(3) funds used for the (c)(4)'s
political activity, and no §501(c)(3) resources (mailing lists, facilities, staff time)
transferred below cost.

Note two things the fiction can use:
- A **§501(c)(4) has no donor disclosure** to the public. This is the "dark money"
  channel, and it is the reason the Campaign Legal Center intervened in *NRB v. Long*.
- The Church's **congregation is a mailing list**, and the list itself has enormous
  political value. Transferring or licensing it to the (c)(4) at fair market value is
  lawful, requires a valuation, and creates a paper trail.

## 5. Lobbying (as distinct from campaigning)

`[SETTLED]` §501(c)(3) organizations may lobby, subject to the "no substantial part"
test. Churches **cannot** make the §501(h) expenditure-test election (§501(h)(5)),
so they are stuck with the vague facts-and-circumstances "substantial part" standard —
generally understood by practitioners as roughly 5% of activities, though no authority
fixes a number.

This is one of the few places where church status is *worse* than ordinary charity
status: a §501(c)(3) that elects §501(h) gets a bright-line safe harbor with a sliding
scale up to $1,000,000 of lobbying expenditures. A church gets ambiguity.

`[EXTRAPOLATED]` For an entity that needs to shape energy siting law, utility
interconnection rules, zoning enabling acts, special-district legislation and AI
regulation (components H and J), lobbying capacity is far more valuable than
endorsement capacity. **The optimizer wants the §501(c)(4), and it wants it for
lobbying, not for elections.** The Johnson Amendment fight — the thing everyone watches
— is a sideshow relative to the ability to write a special district into a state statute
(component H's Ave Maria model).

## (a) The optimizer's likely choice

Scrupulous, visible, documented compliance with §501(c)(3) at the church level. No
endorsements, ever, regardless of what Treasury guidance eventually permits — because
the Church's entire strategic position depends on being read as a religious
institution rather than a political faction, and because *Branch Ministries* proves the
IRS will act when handed a full-page newspaper advertisement.

All political and lobbying capacity in a §501(c)(4) affiliate with a genuinely separate
board, funded by the taxable subsidiaries and by direct (nondeductible) contributions
rather than by church grants. Where the (c)(4) needs the congregation's attention, it
buys the list at appraised value and papers it.

Then spend the capacity almost entirely on **state legislative work**: special-district
enabling legislation, utility interconnection, water rights, zoning. That is where the
architecture's real dependencies live, and none of it makes the news.

## (d) Narrative-usable details

- **One.** The number of churches that have lost exemption for politicking since 1954.
  The Church at Pierce Creek, Binghamton, New York, for a *USA Today* ad in 1992.
- **"Christian Beware."** The actual headline. It cost a church its exemption and made
  constitutional law.
- **Good Friday, April 3, 2026.** Treasury announced its church-speech guidance on Good
  Friday. Somebody chose that date.
- The IRS's own analogy in the July 2025 filing: congregational political speech is like
  **"a family discussion concerning candidates."** An agency describing a congregation
  as a family in a federal court filing.
- **§501(h)(5).** Churches are the only §501(c)(3) organizations forbidden from electing
  the safe harbor that would tell them how much lobbying is too much. The most protected
  entity in the Code is the one kept most deliberately in the dark about its own limits.
- The real prize is not the pulpit. It is a paragraph in a state special-district
  enabling act that nobody reads.
