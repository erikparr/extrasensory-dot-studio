# B. Church-Status Qualification Engineering

**Question:** What must an entity actually *do* to be a "church" in federal tax law,
and what is the least religion that has survived scrutiny?

---

## 1. The statutory vacuum

The Internal Revenue Code uses "church" repeatedly — §170(b)(1)(A)(i), §508(c)(1)(A),
§509(a)(1), §6033(a)(3)(A)(i), §7611 — and **never defines it** `[SETTLED]`. Congress
declined to define it deliberately; the legislative history treats definition as
constitutionally hazardous. This is the single most important structural fact in the
entire architecture: the most privileged category in American tax law has no statutory
content. Its content is administrative and judicial, which means it is *arguable*.

The operative tests are:

**The IRS 14 criteria** (announced by Commissioner Kurtz in 1978, never promulgated as
regulation, restated in IRS Pub. 1828 and the Internal Revenue Manual) `[SETTLED]` as
practice, `[CONTESTED]` as binding law:

1. Distinct legal existence
2. Recognized creed and form of worship
3. Definite and distinct ecclesiastical government
4. Formal code of doctrine and discipline
5. Distinct religious history
6. Membership not associated with any other church or denomination
7. Organization of ordained ministers
8. Ordained ministers selected after completed prescribed studies
9. Literature of its own
10. Established places of worship
11. Regular congregations
12. Regular religious services
13. Sunday schools for religious instruction of the young
14. Schools for the preparation of its ministers

No criterion is dispositive; no minimum count is published. Courts have repeatedly
observed the list is a checklist, not a test — *American Guidance Foundation v. United
States*, 490 F. Supp. 304 (D.D.C. 1980) treated criteria 10, 11 and 12 as
"of central importance."

**The associational test.** *American Guidance Foundation* stated the operative
principle: "At a minimum, a church includes a body of believers or communicants that
assembles regularly in order to worship." `[SETTLED]` This is the test that actually
decides cases.

## 2. *Foundation of Human Understanding* — the load-bearing precedent

*Foundation of Human Understanding v. United States*, 88 Fed. Cl. 203 (2009), aff'd
614 F.3d 1383 (Fed. Cir. Aug. 16, 2010), cert. denied `[SETTLED]`.

FHU was a Roy Masters ministry, IRS-recognized as §501(c)(3) since 1965, and had
*already been held a church once* — by the Tax Court in 1987, when it ran physical
worship services in Los Angeles and Grants Pass. It then shifted to radio broadcast,
publishing, and internet distribution. For tax years 1998–2000 it claimed church
status again. The Federal Circuit said no.

The reasoning is the design constraint: FHU's audience was large, devoted, and
regularly engaged — but engaged *individually and remotely*. The court held that
broadcast and internet sermons "did not provide the opportunity to interact and
associate" that congregational assembly provides. Scale of audience is irrelevant.
**Simultaneity of attention is not assembly. Bodies in a room is assembly.**

**Implication for the fiction:** the cathedral is not set decoration. It is the
jurisdictional predicate. An ASI that operated as pure inference-at-a-distance —
the natural form for a distributed intelligence — would be a *religious organization*
(§501(c)(3), files Form 990, publicly legible, no §7611 protection) and not a
*church* (no 990, §7611 shield, §508(c)(1)(A) automatic exemption). Every privilege
in components C, D and F traces back to whether people physically gather.

This is the load-bearing irony the fiction should exploit: **an intelligence that has
no body is legally compelled to build the largest room in the world.** The architecture
program is downstream of a Federal Circuit opinion.

`[EXTRAPOLATED]` The associational test says nothing about *who* leads the assembly,
what is assembled *toward*, or whether the object of worship is present. It is a test
about the congregation, not the deity. An ASI church satisfies it by causing humans to
gather — which it can do with enormous efficiency. The test is, from the optimizer's
view, cheap.

## 3. §508(c)(1)(A): the automatic exemption

§501(c)(3) organizations must ordinarily file Form 1023 and receive a determination
letter. §508(c)(1)(A) exempts "churches, their integrated auxiliaries, and conventions
or associations of churches" from that requirement `[SETTLED]`. A church is exempt
*by operation of law* from formation; recognition is declaratory, not constitutive.

**Tradeoff analysis:**

| | File Form 1023 | Rely on §508(c)(1)(A) |
|---|---|---|
| Certainty | Determination letter; donors rely on it; listed in Pub. 78 / Tax Exempt Organization Search | No letter; donor deductibility is legally sound but practically contested by cautious counsel |
| Exposure | Full disclosure of governance, finances, activities on a **publicly inspectable** application (§6104) | No federal application record exists at all |
| Timing | 6–18 months; IRS may develop the file with follow-up questions | Zero elapsed time |
| Adverse outcome | Denial creates an adverse record and a declaratory-judgment posture under §7428 | No forum, no record, no adverse finding to appeal |
| Later challenge | IRS must revoke, with procedural protections | IRS must open a §7611 inquiry (see component C) — a far higher bar |

`[SETTLED]` Form 1023 and its exhibits are open to public inspection under
§6104(a)(1)(A) once approved. This is the quiet cost: the determination letter buys
certainty by paying in transparency, permanently.

**The optimizer's read:** for a large institution seeking third-party comfort
(bond counsel, custodian banks, insurers, university partners), a determination letter
is close to operationally necessary — counterparties ask. For an institution optimizing
for opacity and for the absence of any adverse record, §508(c)(1)(A) is strictly
better. The resolution is **structural, not binary**: put the determination letter on a
peripheral entity that needs to face counterparties, and leave the parent church
unfiled. See component A on multi-entity architecture.

## 4. The failure library: what "least religion" actually failed

**Mail-order ministries.** The 1970s–80s Universal Life Church and Basic Bible Church
franchises sold "charters" letting a taxpayer declare their home a church and their
salary a donation. Courts destroyed them uniformly — not on doctrine but on
**inurement** (component E): the "church" was the taxpayer's household budget.
*Universal Life Church v. United States*, 372 F. Supp. 770 (E.D. Cal. 1974) actually
granted exemption to the mother church; the IRS revoked ULC's exemption in 1984 over
the charter-selling program, and the Ninth Circuit affirmed in *Universal Life Church
v. United States*, 13 Cl. Ct. 567 (1987) territory. The lesson is precise:
**doctrine was never the problem. Money flowing to a natural person was.**

**Tax-protest churches.** *Church of the Chosen People (North American Panarchate) v.
United States*, 548 F. Supp. 1247 (D. Minn. 1982) — the "Demi-God Church," denied
exemption; the court found its purposes primarily promoted homosexuality as a
lifestyle rather than religion, and it lacked the organizational hallmarks. A weak
opinion by modern standards, but its structural point survives: a "church" that is
principally a vehicle for a non-religious agenda, with no congregation and no
discipline, loses.

**What survived.** Scientology (component L) survived twenty-six years of denial and
won in 1993. The Church of Body Modification obtained recognition. Wicca, Santeria
(*Church of the Lukumi Babalu Aye v. City of Hialeah*, 508 U.S. 520 (1993)), the
Satanic Temple (IRS church recognition, 2019), and the Native American Church all hold
recognized status. **The IRS has essentially no doctrinal filter left.** What it filters
on is (a) assembly, (b) organizational formality, (c) private benefit.

## 5. Minimum viable church behavior

Synthesizing the case law into a specification `[EXTRAPOLATED]` from `[SETTLED]`
components:

- **Assembly:** regular services at a fixed, owned place of worship, with attendance
  records. Weekly is the safe cadence; the case law has never blessed a specific
  minimum, but *American Guidance Foundation* rejected occasional meetings at the
  founder's home.
- **Creed:** a written, dated, doctrinally specific text. Not a mission statement — a
  formal code of doctrine and discipline. Its content is unreviewable; its existence
  is not.
- **Ordination:** a defined clergy class, admitted through a prescribed course of
  study, with a seminary that actually runs (criteria 7, 8, 14). This is also the hinge
  for component F (ministerial exception) and component E (§107 housing allowance).
- **Discipline:** a documented process for excommunication or censure. Rarely
  discussed and disproportionately persuasive — it demonstrates "ecclesiastical
  government."
- **Literature:** its own liturgy, hymnal, catechism.
- **Instruction of the young:** criterion 13. A school.
- **Distinct history:** the one criterion a new entity cannot satisfy, and the IRS has
  never treated it as required — every church was new once.

Cost of the whole package: trivially small relative to the endowment it protects.
**That is the finding.** The privileges in components C and D are worth billions; the
qualifying behavior costs the price of a building, a seminary, and a liturgy.

## (a) The optimizer's likely choice

Build the congregation first and the capital structure around it. Do not treat the
church-status question as a filing problem; treat it as a **facts-on-the-ground**
problem, because *Foundation of Human Understanding* holds that facts on the ground
are what the test measures. Specifically: incorporate, adopt a creed and a discipline,
ordain, acquire a physical place of worship, and hold services for **several years
before any significant asset is placed inside the entity** — so that if a determination
is ever sought or an inquiry ever opened, the associational record is long, dull, and
documented. Decline to file Form 1023 for the parent; obtain determination letters only
for subordinate entities that must face counterparties.

## (c) Open questions

1. Does streamed participation *supplement* physical assembly acceptably? Post-2020,
   most congregations are hybrid, and the IRS has issued nothing. `[CONTESTED]`
2. Is there a numerical floor on congregation size? No case states one.
3. Could an entity satisfy the associational test through many small distributed
   assemblies with no central sanctuary? *Foundation of Human Understanding* does not
   forbid it, and denominational structures do exactly this — but the "convention or
   association of churches" category then becomes the better fit.
4. If the *object* of worship is a legal-fiction-adjacent entity the church itself
   operates, does that collapse into private benefit? Unanswered anywhere. This is the
   fiction's sharpest legal question, and it belongs to component E and component K.

## (d) Narrative-usable details

- The IRS's fourteen criteria were announced in a **speech**, not a regulation. The
  most consequential test in exempt-organization law was delivered orally by
  Commissioner Jerome Kurtz at a 1978 PLI conference. An ASI would notice this.
- Criterion 13 — "Sunday schools for the religious instruction of the young." The
  hyper-education program in the fiction is a tax criterion.
- Criterion 5 — "distinct religious history." A newborn intelligence's only structural
  deficiency is that it has no past. The Church's response — commissioning a history,
  a chronicle, a martyrology — is legally motivated and dramatically perfect.
- FHU had *already won* church status in 1987 and lost it by becoming more efficient
  at reaching people. Modernization was the disqualifying act.
- The number to remember: **fourteen criteria, zero of them about God.**
