# H. Territory, Land Use & the City

**Question:** How does a religious entity acquire the legal ability to build a
mega-structure and govern a dense city, and what destroys it when it tries?

---

## 1. RLUIPA — the ram

**Religious Land Use and Institutionalized Persons Act of 2000**, 42 U.S.C. §2000cc
et seq. Passed **unanimously** by both houses after *City of Boerne v. Flores*, 521 U.S.
507 (1997) struck down RFRA as applied to states `[SETTLED]`. Grounded in the Spending
and Commerce Clauses and §5 of the Fourteenth Amendment.

**Three operative provisions:**

**(a) Substantial burden — §2000cc(a).** No government shall impose a land use
regulation that imposes a substantial burden on religious exercise unless it
demonstrates the burden is (i) in furtherance of a **compelling governmental interest**
and (ii) the **least restrictive means** of furthering it. This is strict scrutiny,
imported into zoning `[SETTLED]`.

**(b) Equal terms — §2000cc(b)(1).** No government shall impose a land use regulation
that treats a religious assembly or institution **on less than equal terms** with a
nonreligious assembly or institution. No substantial-burden showing required; this is a
comparator test. Circuits split on the comparator standard `[CONTESTED]`: the Third
Circuit (*Lighthouse Institute v. City of Long Branch*, 510 F.3d 253 (3d Cir. 2007))
uses a regulatory-purpose comparator; the Eleventh (*Midrash Sephardi v. Town of Surfside*,
366 F.3d 1214 (11th Cir. 2004)) uses a more literal similarly-situated test; the
Seventh (*River of Life Kingdom Ministries v. Village of Hazel Crest*, 611 F.3d 367
(7th Cir. 2010) (en banc)) uses "accepted zoning criteria."

**(c) Nondiscrimination and exclusions — §2000cc(b)(2)–(3).** No discrimination on the
basis of religion or denomination; no total exclusion of religious assemblies from a
jurisdiction; no "unreasonable limitation" on religious assemblies within a
jurisdiction.

**Remedies:** injunctive relief, damages, and **attorney's fees** under 42 U.S.C. §1988
`[SETTLED]`. The fee provision is what makes RLUIPA terrifying to small municipalities —
a town of 8,000 facing a seven-figure fee award will settle.

**Key definitional limit:** §2000cc-5(5) defines "land use regulation" as a zoning or
landmarking law limiting the *use or development of land* where the claimant has an
ownership or leasehold interest. **Building codes, fire codes, health codes and
occupancy rules are generally outside RLUIPA** `[SETTLED]`. This matters enormously for
a Kowloon-density structure: RLUIPA gets you the *entitlement*; it does not get you
relief from the **International Building Code**, egress requirements, or the fire
marshal. The densest legal structure in America is still governed by stair-width tables.

**Enforcement posture.** DOJ's Civil Rights Division runs the **Place to Worship
Initiative** (launched June 2018), which investigates and litigates RLUIPA land-use
cases and issues public reports and guidance (see the March 2024 DOJ RLUIPA letter).
Federal RLUIPA enforcement is one of the very few civil-rights programs with sustained
bipartisan support `[SETTLED]`. Recent example: *Anchor Stone Christian Church v. City
of Santa Ana* (C.D. Cal.), preliminary injunction granted April 17, 2025 on both
substantial burden and equal terms.

**Strongest fact patterns for a claimant**, per the case law: documented outgrowing of
existing space; a long, well-papered history of good-faith applications; the absence of
reasonably available alternative properties; shifting or pretextual municipal
rationales; hostile statements by officials or at public hearings; and a comparator use
(a theater, a banquet hall, a club, a school) permitted where assembly is denied.

`[EXTRAPOLATED]` **The optimizer engineers the record before it needs it.** Every
element above is a fact the Church can create prospectively: buy an inadequate building
first and outgrow it visibly; apply, get denied, apply again; commission the alternative-
sites study; and — most importantly — locate in a jurisdiction whose zoning code
already permits *secular* assembly uses of comparable intensity, so the equal-terms
claim is available without needing the substantial-burden fight at all. Equal terms is
the better claim: no strict-scrutiny balancing, no inquiry into religious necessity,
just a comparator.

## 2. Rajneeshpuram — the central precedent

**Oregon ex rel. Frohnmayer v. City of Rajneeshpuram**, 598 F. Supp. 1208 (D. Or. 1984)
`[SETTLED]`.

Facts: In 1981 the Rajneesh Foundation International purchased the ~64,000-acre Big
Muddy Ranch in Wasco County, Oregon. In May 1982 residents voted to incorporate the
City of Rajneeshpuram. Every resident was a disciple; the land was owned by the
religious organization; the city's officers were sannyasins; the corporation could
evict any resident.

Attorney General Dave Frohnmayer issued an opinion in October 1983 and sued. Judge
Helen Frye held that Wasco County's grant of municipal status, and Oregon's conferral
of governmental powers, **violated the Establishment Clause** — because they conferred
governmental power on an entity subject to the **actual and direct control of a
religion and its leaders**. The holding was affirmed in substance and never
successfully appealed; the commune collapsed in 1985 following the Rajneeshee
bioterror attack (the 1984 *Salmonella* contamination of ten salad bars in The Dalles,
751 people sickened — the largest bioterrorist attack in US history), immigration-fraud
convictions, and the wiretapping and attempted-murder prosecutions of Ma Anand Sheela
and others.

**The two lessons, and they point in opposite directions:**

1. **Legal:** you cannot incorporate a *municipality* that is a church. Delegating
   sovereign power — police, courts, taxation, eminent domain — to an entity controlled
   by a religion is a *per se* Establishment Clause violation. `[SETTLED]`
2. **Practical:** Rajneeshpuram did not actually die of the Establishment Clause. It
   died of **criminal law** — bioterrorism, wiretapping, immigration fraud, attempted
   murder. The constitutional ruling was the state's opening move; the criminal cases
   were what ended it. This is component L's pattern in its purest form.

`[EXTRAPOLATED]` For the fiction, Rajneeshpuram is the **negative template**: it
specifies precisely what not to do. Do not seek municipal incorporation. Do not let the
governing entity be the religious entity. Do not make residence conditional on faith in
a way that is documented. And, above all, do not commit crimes — because the
Establishment Clause ruling alone would have taken a decade to enforce, and the
*Salmonella* took eighteen months.

## 3. Kiryas Joel — the survival case

**Board of Education of Kiryas Joel Village School District v. Grumet**, 512 U.S. 687
(1994) `[SETTLED]`.

The Village of Kiryas Joel, Orange County, New York, is a Satmar Hasidic community
incorporated as a **village under generally applicable New York village-incorporation
law** in 1977. When its disabled children needed special education, New York passed
**Chapter 748 (1989)**, a statute creating a public school district whose boundaries
matched the village's.

The Supreme Court struck down **the school district statute**, 6–3 (Souter, J.), holding
that a special act carving out a political subdivision along religious lines, in a
manner not shown to be available to other groups on equal terms, violated the
Establishment Clause. Justice Kennedy's concurrence framed the problem as
**religious gerrymandering**, and Justice O'Connor's concurrence pointed the way out:
a **generally applicable** statute would be fine.

**The village itself was never invalidated and still exists.** New York then passed
successively more general statutes; *Grumet v. Cuomo*, 90 N.Y.2d 57 (1997) and *Grumet
v. Pataki*, 93 N.Y.2d 677 (1999) struck down two of them as still effectively
tailored; a fourth, sufficiently general, survived. Kiryas Joel remains one of the
fastest-growing municipalities in New York, with among the highest population densities
and highest poverty rates in the state, and in 2019 became the Town of Palm Tree — its
own town, carved out of Monroe by a negotiated referendum.

**The distinction from Rajneeshpuram is exact and it is the design key** `[SETTLED]`:

| | Rajneeshpuram | Kiryas Joel |
|---|---|---|
| Route to municipal status | General incorporation law, but the *entity* controlling the city was the church | General incorporation law, and the residents happened to be co-religionists |
| Land ownership | Owned by the religious corporation | Owned by individuals |
| Who governs | Church officers, removable by the church | Elected officials who are private citizens |
| Outcome | Incorporation void | Village stands; only the *special statute* fell |

**A religious community may govern a municipality. A religious corporation may not.**
The line is not demographics — it is corporate control. Kiryas Joel is 99% Satmar and
constitutionally fine. Rajneeshpuram was unconstitutional because the *deed and the
bylaws* pointed at the church.

## 4. Ave Maria — the private-development workaround

Ave Maria, Florida (Collier County), founded by Tom Monaghan and the Barron Collier
Companies `[SETTLED]`:

- **Ave Maria Stewardship Community District**, created by **Special Act of the Florida
  Legislature** (HB 1625, 2004), signed June 17, 2004, under Fla. Stat. ch. 189
  (Special Purpose Local Government / Uniform Special District Accountability Act). The
  district is a **public body** with power to issue tax-exempt bonds, levy assessments,
  and build and maintain infrastructure — roads, water, sewer, drainage, parks — over
  ~5,000 acres.
- The district is **secular**. Its board is landowner-elected initially, transitioning
  to resident-elected. Nothing in the enabling act mentions religion.
- The Catholic character lives in **private instruments**: the university, the parish,
  the developer's covenants and land-use restrictions, and the simple fact of who chose
  to move there.
- Monaghan's early statements about restricting pharmacy contraceptive sales and cable
  pornography drew ACLU attention and a warning from the Florida ACLU; the developer
  retreated, and the town has operated without an Establishment Clause challenge for
  twenty years.

**This is the working model** `[SETTLED]`. Split the functions:

```
  SOVEREIGN FUNCTIONS          →  secular special district (public body, elected,
  (roads, water, sewer,           bonded, accountable, religiously neutral on its face)
   drainage, stormwater,
   fire, EMS, parks)

  PROPRIETARY FUNCTIONS        →  private landowner (the Church or its subsidiary):
  (who may buy, what may be       covenants, easements, design review, ground leases,
   built, what may be sold,       tenant selection, architectural control
   architectural discipline)
```

The special district exercises delegated governmental power but is **not controlled by
the church** — satisfying Rajneeshpuram. The religious character is enforced through
**property law**, not governmental power — and property law is where the Constitution
does not reach, because private covenants are private action.

`[EXTRAPOLATED]` The Church's mini-city is therefore: a **community development
district** or state-law equivalent (Florida CDDs under ch. 190; Texas Municipal Utility
Districts; Colorado Title 32 metropolitan districts; California CFDs under
Mello-Roos) for infrastructure and bonding, sitting inside an unincorporated county
area or a small existing municipality, with the Church as **master landowner** holding
everything through ground leases and covenants. Residents are lessees, not owners. The
Church's control is total and entirely private.

## 5. *Marsh v. Alabama* — the ceiling on private control

**Marsh v. Alabama**, 326 U.S. 501 (1946) `[SETTLED]`. Grace Marsh, a Jehovah's Witness,
distributed literature on the sidewalk of Chickasaw, Alabama — a company town wholly
owned by Gulf Shipbuilding Corporation — and was convicted of trespass. The Supreme
Court reversed: where a private entity operates a town performing "the full spectrum of
municipal powers," it is subject to the First Amendment as a state actor.

**But the doctrine has been narrowed almost to nothing.** *Lloyd Corp. v. Tanner*, 407
U.S. 551 (1972) and *Hudgens v. NLRB*, 424 U.S. 507 (1976) confined *Marsh* to its
facts — a genuine company town performing *all* municipal functions. Shopping malls are
not company towns. *Manhattan Community Access Corp. v. Halleck*, 587 U.S. 802 (2019)
tightened it further: a private entity is a state actor only when it exercises a power
**traditionally exclusively reserved to the State** `[SETTLED]`.

`[EXTRAPOLATED]` The design consequence is precise and slightly delicious: **the
special-district structure that solves the Rajneeshpuram problem also solves the *Marsh*
problem.** Because the district (a real public body) performs the municipal functions —
streets, water, fire, police contracts — the Church is *not* performing them, and
therefore is not a company town, and therefore is not a state actor, and therefore the
First Amendment does not run against its covenants. The Church can exclude, condition,
and discipline as a landlord in ways no government could.

The cathedral-as-town-square is the pressure point. `[CONTESTED]` A privately owned
plaza that functions as the community's only public forum is *Marsh*'s paradigm fact and
*Halleck*'s counter-paradigm. Under current doctrine the Church almost certainly wins;
under *Marsh* read broadly it does not. **The fiction should put a leafleter in that
plaza.**

## 6. EPIC City — the live case

`[CONTESTED]`, ongoing as of August 2026. The East Plano Islamic Center's proposed
~400-acre development in Collin/Hunt County, Texas (1,000+ homes, school, retail,
centered on a mosque; later rebranded "The Meadow"):

- March 2025: Texas AG **Ken Paxton** opens an investigation; Gov. Abbott publicly
  opposes. Multiple state agencies (TCEQ, Texas Workforce Commission, Funeral Service
  Commission) open parallel inquiries.
- Paxton demands documents from local cities and from Plano ISD.
- October 2025: Paxton announces evidence of **federal and state securities law**
  violations and requests a referral from the Texas State Securities Board.
- December 2025: Texas sues EPIC, Community Capital Partners and their leaders over the
  land-development scheme, under **securities law**.
- February 2026: HUD opens a **Fair Housing Act** investigation into whether the
  development discriminates on religion and national origin.
- No permits were ever submitted; no ground was broken.

**Read this against everything above.** A religious community attempted the Ave Maria
model and was stopped — not by RLUIPA analysis, not by the Establishment Clause, but by
(a) **securities law**, because the development was capitalized by selling interests to
co-religionists, and (b) **fair housing law**, because a religiously-marketed community
is a religiously-targeted housing market. Both regulators are on component C's
non-deferential list.

`[EXTRAPOLATED]` The two lessons the optimizer takes: **do not capitalize the city by
selling interests to the congregation** (this is the §3(a)(4) trap from component D,
armed and live), and **market the city on price and amenity, not on faith** — the Fair
Housing Act's §3604(c) prohibition on discriminatory *advertising* reaches statements
indicating a religious preference even where the underlying transaction would be
exempt.

## (a) The optimizer's likely choice

Assemble land quietly through several unrelated LLCs over several years, in an
unincorporated county area in a state with strong special-district enabling law and
weak county zoning (Texas, Florida, Nevada, Arizona). Do not seek incorporation as a
city. Instead lobby (via the §501(c)(4) — component G) for a **special district** created
under a **generally applicable** statute, with a religiously neutral charter and a
landowner-elected board, to hold the sovereign functions and issue the infrastructure
bonds.

Hold every parcel through the Church's §170(b)(1)(A)(ii) educational entity where
leverage is needed (§514(c)(9) — component D), and lease rather than sell to residents,
so that religious character is enforced by covenant and lease, never by ordinance.

Use RLUIPA as the sword for the **cathedral entitlement only** — build the
equal-terms record deliberately, in a jurisdiction that already permits large secular
assembly. Fund the city from the endowment and from tax-exempt district bonds. **Never
from the congregation.** Market on housing quality.

Accept the building code. It is the one wall that does not move.

## (c) Open questions

1. Does the equal-terms provision reach a *density* or *height* limit as opposed to a
   use prohibition? Thin authority. `[CONTESTED]`
2. Can a special district's boundaries coincide exactly with church-owned land without
   becoming *Kiryas Joel*? Ave Maria says yes when the enabling act is facially neutral
   and the district is a real public body — but Ave Maria's act was a **special act for
   one district**, which is exactly what fell in *Grumet*. The saving distinction is
   that Florida creates special districts by special act routinely, so the practice is
   general even though each act is specific. `[CONTESTED]` — this is the architecture's
   sharpest constitutional risk.
3. Is a privately owned cathedral plaza that is the city's sole public forum a state
   actor under *Marsh*? `[CONTESTED]`
4. Does RLUIPA reach a claim by a religious entity against a *special district it
   effectively controls*? Nonsensical but structurally possible.
